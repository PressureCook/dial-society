Dials model · forty sixth simulation

Dial Society

A society that starts with 120 simulated agents in six communities, on land where people with no AI already live, and grows for as long as the land can feed it. Nothing else caps it: when a community has more people than its food can feed, the weakest starve. Everyone lives somewhere on a real map, of the world or of a part of it, with real coastlines and real resource regions, and the closer an AI lives to its community, family and alliance the more power it has. Every one is a small learning AI that seeks the highest score it can. Score doubles with every equal share of power an AI holds, is multiplied by how many people are alive, and feeling bad loses a little, and an AI feels bad unless it holds well over an equal share of power. Each generation the agents imagine life under slightly different rules, learn which direction would leave them with a higher score, and push the rules of their own society that way, as hard as their own power allows. Nobody tells them what any rule is for. A newborn spends a generation as a child, and only someone who has been an adult for a generation can have children. An adult has forty encounters a generation, every one at the full stake. In each it can deal fairly or cheat, share, give to someone stronger, buy or sell food and other resources at a price the two settle between them, ask the other for whatever it needs, take by force, or kill. Whoever it wrongs punishes it by their own rule, and the hardest punishers can put it out of the community or put it to death. The less power someone holds, the likelier all of this is to happen to them. A gift buys influence over whoever receives it. Influence counts for three times as much as power when something is asked for, and it adds to power. Resources come in three kinds: those that lengthen life, which are also the only ones that feed anyone, those that shorten it, and those that can do either. An invention is made from one kind and is of no use without it, and every resource runs down the more it is worked. There is no limit on the number of communities, and someone put out of theirs belongs to none. What people hold carries on to their heirs. About half of every generation is born with a head start in power, and someone with a head start can only have a child with someone who has none. AIs can keep their alliance secret or lie about it. One rule is a limit on power: at 0 resources add no power at all, and at 100 a single AI could hold all of its community's. Every AI also has its own rules for how hard it punishes, teaches conscience and stokes wanting. The rules of the society only sway the own rules that children grow up with. Children can die young, less often the more inventions that lengthen life their community has put to use. On Rapa Nui the ground is the real terrain of the island, and steep ground costs more to cross. The people with no AI are not agents. They hold no power, deal with nobody and belong to no community. They hold land of their own, eat from it and have children, and a child of one of them and an AI is an AI. Everything this page says of people is about the AIs unless it says otherwise.

Head start and no head start

About half of every generation, founders and children alike, is born with a head start worth one and a half times the power of the rest. It is luck and is not handed down, and someone who has one can only have a child with someone who has none. Feeling runs from −1 to +1.

Own rules

Every AI has its own rule for how hard it punishes, how hard it teaches conscience and how hard it stokes wanting. The overarching rule is the society's. It only sways the own rules children grow up with. Each parent then adjusts its own rules toward whatever left its children with the higher score. Own rules also work on other adults: whoever you wrong punishes you and teaches you conscience by their own rules, and whoever you deal fairly with stokes your wanting by theirs. What an AI punishes is its own choice. Anything can be punished, good acts included. It holds a weight from 0 to 1 for each of thirteen acts: cheating, robbery, killing, breaking a promise or a debt, sabotage, lying, refusing to deal, using inventions, dealing fairly, trading, giving, inventing and having children. It punishes an act with a chance equal to its punishment rule times its weight on that act times what its power allows, and punishing costs it a little too. Acts done to it, it sees for itself. Acts anyone might do in the open, such as a fair deal, a trade, a gift, using inventions, inventing or having children, are seen by one other member of the actor's community, or a neighbour, one time in five, and that one punishes as its own weights say. An AI expects the punishment it would face for dealing fairly, so where fair dealing is punished, cheating looks better by comparison. The weights are handed down, copied from those doing better, and learned together: in the imagined futures each family tries all thirteen a little up or down at once, each its own way, and keeps the directions that left it and its line better off. A community punishes by its culture times the power weighted average of its members' weights. When someone kills, a few of the killer's own people hear of it, and each punishes it if it chooses to punish killing, so even a community with no rivals has a check on its killers if its members want one. An own punishment rule above 0.7 is at the high end, where a punishment can go as far as exile or death.

The communities

Each community keeps norms of its own for punishment, conscience and wanting, set by its own most powerful members. They matter more as community weight rises. There is no limit on how many communities there can be, and the people who belong to none are listed last, as a group. Children is the share of members still in their childhood. Can feed is how many people the food resources in its reach keep from hunger, and Food bought or sold is what its members traded with members of other communities this generation, counted in people fed. Resources in reach is the community's share of everything in the ground, of all three kinds. It follows where its people live and how many they are. Inventions covered says how far the resources in its reach cover what its members' inventions need: first for inventions made from resources that lengthen life, then for those made from the means of harm, then for those made from resources that can do either. Below 100%, every invention of that kind counts for that much less. Closeness is how near its members live to their community, family and alliance, from 0% for far away to 100% for right beside them. Life is how many generations an adult life lasts in that community. How it works, once per generation

  1. The goal.
    score each round = (2 ^ (power held ÷ power of those within reach) − 1) × AIs alive ÷ 120 ± feeling
    Every AI seeks one thing, the highest score for itself and its line. Each round it gains score for the power it holds, measured against the people within its reach: its power over the average power of those around it, the nearer each and the more powerful, the more it counts. That score grows exponentially: it doubles with every share of the local average it holds, up to 60 doublings. Its place in the order of power, which makes it a likelier or less likely target and shapes how others see it, is likewise its place among those within its reach. That score is then multiplied by how many AIs are around: the number alive divided by the 120 the society started with, people with no AI not counted, so the same power is worth five times as much on a full board and almost nothing in a society of ten. It also gains score from its descendants, which is the only reason it has to want children: every round a parent gains a share of what each of its living children gained, and a grandparent a share of what each grandchild gained. How large each share is, and whether there is one at all, is its own: partly from its parents and partly chance, with one newborn in six caring nothing for its line and some caring less than nothing. It is not fixed. Each life it moves a tenth of the way toward the average of its community, and the weight on children a tenth toward its alliance's, and in every encounter whoever has influence over it pulls its weights toward its own. It loses score by how bad it feels and gains score by how good it feels. Feeling good or bad is part chance and part what happens: each AI has a mood that keeps part of itself from one round to the next and is otherwise drawn at random; gaining power lifts it and losing power lowers it, by the logarithm of the change; working hard weighs on it; and the inventions it knows can lift or lower it. The feelings lever scales how much score a bad feeling costs. What a feeling does is chance too. Each AI is born with a random push of its feelings on three things, each of which may go either way: on its wanting when a chance to cheat, rob or kill comes up, on its readiness to share, and on its readiness to leave its alliance. How far it acts on its feelings is learned, like its other own rules, and its dials carry each push: wanting carries the push on wanting, guilt the push on sharing, and fear the push on leaving.
  2. Training.
    lesson = score with the rule nudged up − score with it nudged down, remembered as a push from −1 to +1
    Before each generation, every agent imagines being born again into its own family under one rule nudged up and nudged down, with the same luck both times. The difference tells it which way gives it the higher score. An imagined life is a coarser sketch than a real one, to keep the imagining affordable: it skips childhood, and each round it has one encounter that stands for five. Foresight is personal and counted in days. Every AI is born with a random amount of it, and every encounter in its life adds a little, so the old and the well travelled see furthest. The Foresight setting is the furthest anyone can look; an AI sees that far times the cube of its own foresight, so many see only years ahead, some only days, and the far sighted whole generations. One that sees less than a generation judges a choice by its imagined score on the day its sight ends. Every horizon is foreseen in twelve steps, so a year is foreseen month by month and a generation about two years at a time; inventions can make the steps finer, and the coarser the step the less sure the prediction, so a lesson can mislead. Every day foreseen takes an action: seeing a generation ahead takes about a sixteenth of all its actions, out of its working time. With foresight the agent does not stop at one imagined life. It follows its line into the generations after: each further generation adds the scores of everyone in its line, itself in full if it is still alive, each child at a quarter, each grandchild at a sixteenth and so on down, or the worst score going if the line has died out. So more descendants count for more, and each step down the line counts for less. Its lesson is the average over the generations it can see. Foresight costs actions, not power. The Foresight setting sets how far the most far sighted can see: every sixth on the setting adds a generation, up to four. Looking further also leaves time to study fewer rules: four each generation when looking one generation ahead, two at two, and one at three or four. What an AI keeps of a lesson is how hard it says to push, from −1 for as hard as possible against the rule to +1 for as hard as possible for it. A lesson says to push most of the way when the full range of the rule is worth one equal share of power to the AI. A child starts from what its parents pushed for, and each new lesson replaces a quarter of what was remembered.
  3. Action.
    push = (1 − fear ÷ 2) × (reward × own lesson + guilt × what those around want) ÷ (reward + guilt)
    What an agent has learned is only where its own score lies. What it does about it is decided by its three dials. The reward dial is how hard it pushes for its own score. The guilt dial is how much it pushes instead for what the people in its community want, taken as their average learned push. The fear dial holds it back from pushing at all, by up to half. Children do not push. Every community sets its own rules. Its value of each rule moves by the sum of its own members' pushes weighted by their power, with each alliance pushing as one bloc. A rule applies to an AI by the community it belongs to: its power limit, how much its alliance lifts it, what it inherits, how much it shares, and so on. A new community starts with the rules of the communities its founders came from. Each parent also tests one of its own three rules every generation, nudged up and nudged down in imagined lives of its children, and moves it a little toward whichever left them with the higher score, further the higher its reward dial and less far the higher its fear dial.
  4. Formation.
    dial = baseline + sensitivity × conditioning
    Every AI has its own rules: how hard it punishes, how hard it teaches conscience and how hard it stokes wanting. A child is conditioned at home by its parents' own rules, and the rest of its upbringing comes from its deals. Parental control sets how much of childhood is spent at home. For the rest, every deal a child takes part in moves its dials and its own rules toward the own rules of the one it dealt with, more so the more power that one holds. Communities, alliances and the society no longer raise children directly; they reach a child only through the people it deals with. Sensitivity is inborn and half inherited. An imagined life skips childhood, so an imagined newborn is raised as if by the people of its community, weighted by power. The first generation of a life is childhood, described under population and families.
  5. Encounters and influence.
    when cheated: chance guilt ÷ 100 conscience grows, chance (100 − guilt) ÷ 100 it shrinks
    Nobody knows everybody. An AI knows another AI, and that AI's alliance, only once the two have had an encounter. A newborn knows its parents and their alliances. It matters in three places: the other parent of a child has to be someone the first one has met, an AI can only join or defect to an alliance it has come across, and a founder can only bring in people it knows. A generation is 25 years of 365 days, lived season by season, so 100 steps. Each day has at most 16 actions, and only in daylight. Dealing and working share them: the share of its day an AI spends meeting people is its willingness to deal, and the rest goes to working, which is extracting and inventing. Inventions can add actions to a day or take some away, by chance, as far as they are produced. Daylight is a full share in summer, about six in seven in spring and autumn, and under two thirds in winter. The ground gives by the season too, a third more in summer and four tenths less in winter, and getting about costs a third more in winter. Encounters have no set number. Before every encounter an adult decides whether to go on meeting people, by a willingness of its own that it learns like its other own rules; once it stops, or can no longer afford to go anywhere, it is done seeking encounters for the generation, though others may still come to it. There is no safety limit: the generation runs its 25 years unless everyone stops or dies first. Everything still costs. What the ground gives, living costs, hunger, inventing and its costs, production and upkeep are each set per season so that a year adds up to what it should, and anything that happens by chance in a step, a death from being unable to pay for living, a dealing with a community, being seen using an invention, has its chance scaled to a season too. Score is counted per step the same way. In three encounters of ten an AI seeks out, among those it knows, whoever is worth most to it: held in regard, rich, or owing it; the rest are with whoever is near. In any encounter, an AI that sees the other doing better this life sometimes copies a little of one of its own rules. Every one counts in full: a whole deal, a whole fare for the trip, a whole punishment if caught. Neighbours meet: most of an AI's dealings are with the ten adults living nearest to it on the map, whatever community they belong to. The rest are with anyone at all, allies seek each other out, and one time in four the richest of three candidates is the one met. Each side cheats or deals fairly by the selfish score. A fair deal is paid out of the new wealth of each side's community, as described under resources. When fair deals claim more than there is, each pays out a smaller share, and the gain from cheating counts for that much more in the score. With forty deals a generation drawing on the same ground, that share is low on a full map. After the deal the better off of the two may share some of the gap with the other: guilt at the gap pulls toward giving, more so between allies, and the reward dial pulls toward keeping. The weaker may give to the stronger, either may sell to the other, and either may ask the other for something, as described under gifts, influence and asking and under trade and raids. Whoever started the deal may also try to take part of what the other holds by force. The chance it works is their share of the two parties' power, so the powerful succeed more and risk less. Wanting pushes toward it, guilt and fear of punishment hold it back. Every wrong done wears the wrongdoer's guilt down, by its sensitivity: each cheat by 2% of what is left, each attempt at force by 5% and each attempt on a life by 15%. The less power someone holds, the likelier bad things are to happen to them: cheating, taking by force and killing are all more tempting against someone near the bottom of the order of power and less tempting against someone near the top. Own rules condition other adults too. Whoever you wrong punishes you by their own punishment rule: as often as the rule says when the two of you are equal in power, more often the more power they hold against you, and less often the less. A punishment is always a fine, and it raises your fear. Above 0.7 an own punishment rule is at the high end, and what it hands out can go further. At the very top, against an equal, one punishment in twenty is death and one in ten puts the wrongdoer out of the community the two share. Both are likelier the more power the punisher holds against the wrongdoer, by up to half again. A punisher at the high end also looms larger for the fear dial, up to three times. To the dead one's own people a death handed out by an outsider is a killing like any other. Whoever you wrong also teaches you conscience: every time you cheat someone or try to take from them, your guilt rises by as much as their own teaching rule says. Whoever you deal fairly with rewards it, and that stokes your wanting by as much as their own reward rule says. Each adult pays the upkeep of its own punishing and teaching, more the more it holds. Every attempt at force, successful or not, cuts the taker's standing among its own for the rest of their life, by up to a fifth, and lowers the target's regard for the taker. How much depends on who is judging: an alliance judges what its members do to outsiders by its own conscience, which is its founder's guilt dial, so an alliance with little guilt shrugs it off. Wronging a fellow member, or acting with no alliance behind you, costs the full fifth. The same scaling applies to the mask slipping when a cheat is caught. When someone is caught and punished, people who know them hear of it, up to four each time. Each sets it against what it does itself and what its own dials make of such things. Someone who cheats and takes thinks the better of a fellow, and someone who does not thinks the worse, by up to a quarter of what being cheated itself would cost in regard. After every deal both move toward what the other shows: the more powerful pull harder, whoever holds influence over the other pulls harder still, the more sensitive move further, and allies move each other most. Being caught raises fear and dulls wanting, and getting away with it does the reverse.
  6. Population and families.
    an invention in a stable generation adds a quarter generation of life; an invention amid many killings takes one away
    The society starts with 20 AIs in each of six communities, all of them adults. There is no limit on the number of communities. Leaving, joining and founding one are a free choice. Once a generation each adult weighs staying, leaving to belong to none, joining one of the three nearest communities, or founding a new one where it lives. Every choice an AI makes also weighs what it expects others to think of it: how far the people of its community approve of taking for oneself, how many will know (the one it was done to and their talk, and whoever nearby notices), and how much the act weighs, counted by its fear. A wrong is worth doing for its reputation where the people around approve of taking for oneself, and costs it where they do not; a good act the other way round. This goes into cheating, force, killing, sabotage, lying, selling what one does not have, cheating in a trade, giving, sharing, pushing into a deal, selling silence, voting for war, leaving a partner or parent, and choosing a community. Leaving is judged by everyone who knows the one leaving, each by how it sees the act, and an AI choosing whether to leave adds up the regard it expects to lose and gain among those it knows: wanting approves of going where one does better, while guilt and fear hold against breaking faith. The people it leaves take it worse, the more so the more guilt they have, and the people it joins take it better. For each it weighs what the community can reach per head, its pooling bonus, the pay of a fair deal, the danger of being killed there by its fear, how far away it is, and how close the community's norms are to its own rules, with a little chance; leaving costs what leaving always costs, and children go with their parent. Whoever founds a community sets its starting norms: the ones it lives by itself, and the rules of its old community moved the way it wanted them moved. An alliance can found a new one: when at least eight of its members live nearer to the alliance's centre than to the centres of their own communities, and the alliance sits apart from those communities, those members leave and become a community of their own, starting with the rules they live by. Leaving a community costs no power. Later members who end up nearer the alliance join it. A community that dies out is gone. Not everyone belongs to a community. Someone put out of theirs, as a punishment or because enough of the community's power backed it, belongs to none and loses power as anyone does who leaves. The children it has from then on belong to none either. People with no community pool no power and have no limit on it, lay only a small claim to land, and nobody answers for killing one of them. Each community holds them in some standing as a group. They get back in by asking a member of a community for a place, or when an alliance they belong to founds a community. A dot on the map is one AI, and it can stand for any number of people: one on Rapa Nui, four million on the map of the world. Every count of people on the page is multiplied by that scale and the dots are drawn bigger the more people each stands for. The population has no cap. What limits it is food, and the food the usable resources can give is the cap on people. Farmland, fishing grounds and fresh water always feed people. Each place of another kind has a 30% chance of feeding people too, from a fifth to four fifths as well, and so may a place an invention uncovers. Each community can feed as many people as the food resources in its reach allow, and lack of them kills. Every round a community with more members than it can feed loses a quarter of the excess to hunger, and the less power a member holds the likelier it is to be one of them, so those with more power are less affected. Whoever has bought food for itself that generation is spared. What the dead held goes to the rest of their community. A life begins with a generation of childhood. A child is raised, eats and can starve, invents nothing and pushes no rule. It deals, as often as its own willingness to meet says, with the adults around its family: plain deals only, fair or cheated, with no force, killing or trade, and each deal raises it. It has a quarter of the power its holdings would give an adult, and a head start does not count until it is grown, so a child is always weaker than an adult with no head start and the same holdings. After that it is an adult, and only someone who has already lived a generation as an adult can have children. Every generation each of those chooses how many children to want, and the founders choose too: before the first children are born, each founder imagines the futures in which everyone wants none, one, two, three or four a generation and takes the number that left it, and its line as far as it can see, with the highest score. A child is a deal. An AI that wants one asks another AI, and the one asked says yes the way any request is answered: by the asker's power against it, three times as much by the asker's influence over it, by its regard for the asker, and by its own wish for children, less those it has already agreed to that generation. Someone asked who says no can be followed by another, up to three, and if all three say no the child is not born. No power is put into children: a child costs its parents only what it eats and needs to live. How many children to have is an AI's own to set, like its own rules. They are handed down from parents to children with a little drift, and every fifth generation each AI tests one of them in imagined lives and moves it toward whichever left it with the higher score. A parent who dies that generation pays out of what it leaves behind. Children are born until their community holds a tenth more people than it can feed. Any more than that die at birth, the children of the more powerful parents are the likelier to live, and only children who live are paid for. Children die young too. Where a community has no invention that lengthens life in use, 45% of its children die before they grow up, which is about what it was everywhere before modern medicine. Every such invention in use lowers that, each counted as far as the community had the resource for it when it came into use: two of them halve it. So does the life-lengthening invention the infant's parents hold: each unit a parent holds counts as much as two inventions in use in the community. The danger is met once, when the infant is born: an infant that dies is off the board, and what it had been given goes back to its parents. It is not tied to any deal. The children of people with no AI meet it at birth too. Families can break up. Once a generation each partner weighs leaving the other, a grown child weighs cutting ties with its parents, and a parent weighs abandoning a newborn. Each is likelier the worse the two think of each other, the more wanting the one leaving has and the less guilt, and a parent who puts more weight on its children is less likely to abandon one. A child cut off no longer counts as its parents' for score, upbringing or inheritance. A child has two parents wherever a second can be found. The one asked is an adult neighbour that the first has met, from any community, and the more powerful a neighbour is the likelier it is to be asked first. Someone with a head start has to have its child with someone who has none. If no neighbour it has met will do, it turns to anyone it has met, then to the nearest adult without a head start, and if there is nobody the child is not born. Sensitivity and what inventions gave come from both parents in equal parts, and both endow the child and leave it an inheritance. A head start is luck: every child has a 50% chance of one, whoever its parents are, just as the founders did. What the child learns is a deal between its parents, settled the way any deal is: each one deals fairly or grabs, by its own reward, fear and guilt dials and the other's punishment rule. If both are fair the child takes half of its lessons and half of the rules it is raised by from each. If one grabs, everything comes from that one. If both grab, nothing is passed on: the child starts with no lessons and is raised by the rules of the people around it. A parent who grabs can be punished by the other, as often as the other's punishment rule says, and how hard it lands scales with the other's power against its own: up to half of what the grabber holds, plus a rise in its fear. The child joins the alliance of whichever parent taught it more, the more powerful one if they taught it equally, and no alliance if neither did. When the parents belong to different communities the child follows the more powerful one, with odds set by their shares of power, and that parent counts as its family line. The child is born between its parents. If that spot is closer to the middle of another community than to the lead parent's own, the child belongs to the other community, unless it is integrated into the lead parent's. The chance of that is the average of four things: how much of the lead parent's alliance lives in the home community, that parent's power against the average of the community, its average respectability in the eyes of those it met, and how well its three dials fit the home community compared with the other one. The child of someone with no community has none. After its childhood a life lasts two generations to begin with, so a parent is still on the board while its children grow up. A living parent hands the children born that generation up to 30% of what it holds, as far as the inheritance rule allows. When it dies of old age its living children inherit, and anything not passed down goes to the community's newborn. Someone who is killed leaves nothing. Staying alive costs resources. Every round each AI alive uses a fiftieth of an average holding, a child half that, paid out of what it holds and gone. One who cannot pay all of it goes short and may die of it: 5% a round for one who can pay nothing, less for one who pays part. Lacking power alone kills nobody. The length of an adult life is kept community by community, and it moves a quarter of a generation at a time, only in a generation in which an invention was made in the community. If that generation was stable, with fewer than one in a hundred of the community's people killed by another, it goes up. If there was more killing than that, it goes down. With no invention it stays where it is. A death handed out as a punishment is not a killing here, and the people a raid kills count, in the generation after the raid. An adult life always lasts between one and four generations.
  7. The people with no AI.
    a child of an AI and someone with no AI is an AI
    The founders do not arrive on empty land. People with no AI live there already: 600 at the default setting, spread evenly over the land, all of them adults. They are not agents. They have no dials, hold no power and nothing of their own, deal with nobody, invent nothing, push no rule and belong to no community or alliance. No AI can cheat, rob or kill one of them, since they hold nothing to take. What they do is live on the land, eat from it and have children. Each lays the claim to land that someone with no community lays, three tenths of a member's, by how near it lives, and between them they hold whatever share of every place that comes to. That land feeds them and nobody else, and no community draws wealth from it. So the more AIs there are, the less land the people with no AI hold. When there are more of them than their land can feed, a quarter of the excess dies each round, as in a community, and they wear down the land that feeds them as anyone does. They live one generation as a child and two as an adult. Nothing anyone invents changes that or saves their children: 45% of those die young. Each adult has one child in every generation of its adult life and an even chance of a second. That is three in a life, six for every two parents, about what people had before birth control. It has each child with one of the ten adults living nearest to it, AI or not, picked at random. Two parents with no AI have a child with none, born between them, as long as their kind numbers no more than a tenth over what its land feeds. A child with one AI parent is an AI, the same as any other: nothing marks it and nothing counts it apart. It is born between the two and is the AI's child as if the AI had had it alone: it joins the community the AI's other children would, is raised by the AI's own rules, inherits from it and is endowed by it like any child. It is a deal like any other child: the AI is asked and decides, by its own wish for children against someone who holds no power and no influence over it, and when it says yes it pays for the child as any parent does. It is born only while the AI's community has room, after the children the AIs chose to have. Someone with a head start who has nobody else to have a child with can have it with the nearest adult with no AI. Each generation the people with no AI who live on drift a little from where they were. The AIs imagine them staying exactly as they are: no imagined life has one of them born, moving or dying, and no AI foresees a child it did not choose. If the last AI dies while people with no AI live on, there will never be another AI, and the page stops.
  8. Resources.
    a community's share of a place = its people × its nearness × its hold, set against everyone else's
    Resources lie where they really are. Each map carries its best known resource regions, and on the abstract square they are twelve random places. Every place is one of three kinds, by what the resource does to life. Farmland, fishing grounds and fresh water lengthen life, and they are the only resources that feed anyone. Obsidian, uranium, sulphur and saltpetre, the raw materials of weapons, shorten it. Stone, metals, coal, oil and gas, and diamonds can do either. Power over a place goes by nearness. Everyone lays a claim on it that falls with how far they live from it, and each place is shared among the communities by the sum of their members' claims, times the hold on it that raids have given or taken. Inside a community, what it holds of a place is split among its members by how near each lives, at the usual reach, and half of a member's power base follows its share of all its community holds against the average member's: from half as much for one far from everything to two and a half times as much for one living on top of it. How far a community reaches grows with its inventions: its range is the usual one times its members' average invention level, which is 1 with no inventions in use and rises as inventions raise their power, up to three times the usual reach. So a community that splits in two holds no more land than it did, and a community far from everything gets little. Someone with no community lays a claim of their own, by how near they live, worth three tenths of a member's. So does every one of the people with no AI, and what those hold is theirs alone. The world's new wealth each round comes from all three kinds and is a fixed amount set by what is left in the ground, whatever the number of people: at the default setting and with the ground untouched, just enough to keep 2,000 people at the edge of hunger, which is half of what an orderly society would create for itself. Each community gets the share that matches the resources in its reach. Its commons takes a cut and its members' fair deals share the rest. Resources run out the more they are used. Each generation a place loses up to 1% of what it holds to ordinary use: for food resources by how full its holders are against what they can feed, and for the others by how much of what their ground gives its holders drew. It loses up to another 1% by how hard its holders' inventions draw on that kind of resource. Farmland, fishing grounds and fresh water grow back, by 3% a generation of the gap to the most they have held. The other kinds do not. No place falls below a twentieth of the most it has held. Resources are finite, the hidden ones too. Hidden in the ground from the start is twice what the visible places hold, and no more. One invention in ten uncovers some of it where its maker lives, about 3% of all that was hidden each time, of any of the three kinds. Close to a known place of that kind it makes that place richer, and further away it opens a new place on the map. Its maker knows a use for it. Once the hidden store is used up, nothing more is found.
  9. Invention.
    chance of inventing each round = 0.2% × (1 − strictness) × (1 + inventions in use ÷ 20)
    Inventions are rare, they cost resources, and they are less rare where they have piled up. Every invention is made from a resource its maker knows a use for, and at the start nobody knows a use for any. Finding one is chance, and rare: every round each adult has a chance in a hundred of discovering a use for a place it did not know, likelier for a place near it and for a rich one. A child knows every use either parent knew, whoever is handed an invention learns of the resource it is made from, and the dead take what they knew with them. Food still feeds people whether or not anyone knows a use for it. At the start of each life every adult chooses what kind of invention to put its resources into, among the kinds it knows, and an adult who knows none puts nothing in until it finds one. It can be one made from one of the three kinds of resource, chosen by how much of each kind its community can reach, pulled toward what lengthens life by its guilt dial and toward weapons by its wanting dial. Or it can be sea travel, pulled, for one that does not know it yet, by how many of its trips water stopped in its last life. Every round it then puts a share of what it holds into that kind of invention: up to a twentieth, by a choice of its own that it learns like its other own rules, by imagining its own future and its children's with a little more and a little less. A quarter of what it has put in is used up each round whether or not anything comes of it. The chance of inventing each round is very small, grows with the number of inventions its community already has in use, up to five times, and grows with what it has in hand, up to three times more. What it has in hand also makes the invention better: an invention is worth the square root of what was in hand against the cost that gives the plain chance, times a random amount. One time in four it turns out worse: it takes from nothing up to that much power from those who know it instead of adding, and inventions that turned out worse can bring an AI's power down to a quarter of what it would be without them. Otherwise it adds from nothing to twice that. Whoever is handed an invention takes it as it is, better or worse. Making one spends everything in hand. Made from a resource that lengthens life it lengthens life. Made from a resource that can do either, it is the inventor's choice, by conscience: it lengthens life with a chance equal to the inventor's guilt dial. One made from a resource that shortens life, a weapon, harms nobody's life: it gives those who know it half as much power again as another invention would. Besides power and getting about, every invention has a chance in three of changing each of four more things in those who know it, either way and by a random amount: how they feel, their wanting, their fear and their guilt. These work every round, as far as the AI produces its inventions: a mood effect lifts or lowers the mood, and a dial effect moves that dial a little each round, up or down. They add up over the inventions an AI knows, and a child inherits half of what its parents' inventions did to it. Sea travel gives no power: it makes crossing water possible. Nobody can go to deal with someone across water, or move house over it, without knowing sea travel. A child knows it if either parent did, and whoever is handed it knows it. Any invention, sea travel included, has a chance in three of changing how easily those who know it get about, and it can go either way: it multiplies how far they go and divides what trips cost by e to a random power between −0.4 and 0.4, so by up to half again or down to two thirds. These add up over the inventions an AI knows, to at most four times as easy or a quarter as easy, and a child inherits half of what its parents' inventions did to it. An invention also has to be produced to be used. Every round each AI pays to produce the inventions it knows: a hundredth of an average holding for each whole multiple of power they make up, counting those that turned out worse and what they do to getting about, and half that again for sea travel. It pays after what it needs to stay alive, and its inventions count only as far as it could pay. Sea travel works only for one that paid at least half. You need the resource to use the invention. What a community's members draw on each kind of resource for their inventions is set against what the resources in its reach can carry, and where that falls short every member's inventions of that kind count for that much less. A member who has bought the resource for itself that generation is spared. The ground as it lay at the start could carry enough invention to triple the power of every person it could feed. The more power it already holds the more the invention is worth to it: its power rises by up to a whole, by a half at an equal share, and by little for someone with almost none. After that an invention only goes where its holders choose to send it, or where someone asks for it and is given it. In every deal, each side picks one invention it holds that the other lacks and decides whether to hand it over, by one test: does it leave the giver with more power. Whatever power the other gains comes out of everyone's share, and the giver loses its own share of all power's worth. Some comes back: through the community's shared pool if the two share a community, through the alliance's pool and the lift a stronger alliance gives its members if they share an alliance, half of that lift if their alliances are allied, and through the couple's pool if they are partners. The giver hands it over only when more comes back than is lost. Whoever receives it holds it too, and makes the same choice with the people it deals with. A copy is worth less than the original: a quarter of what it gave the inventor for the inventor's family, and for anyone else a fifth where they live in peace, falling toward a twentieth where there was killing last generation. Each time, those with more power gain more. An invention counts as in use by its home community once a quarter of that community holds it, and by the world once a quarter of everyone does. One that has not reached the world after three generations stops being passed on. Power is one pool that all the AIs share, so whatever an invention gives comes out of everyone else's share. Inventions stack. Each community keeps a count of the inventions it has in use: its own once a quarter of it holds them, and everyone else's once the world has adopted them. Every twenty in that stack double what a new invention from that community is worth, to the inventor, the family and above all the community. Half of a stack falls out of use each generation. Invention goes community by community: the chance falls as the community's strictness rises. Strictness is half upbringing styles, the average of members' own punishment and conscience teaching rules, and half the community's stats, its members' average fear and guilt dials. Children start with half of what inventions had given their parents, kind by kind. The power limit still applies.
  10. Killing.
    chance a killer is punished = average own punishment rule of the victim's community
    Whoever started a deal may also try to kill the other. It is weighed like taking by force, with a bigger prize and a much heavier weight on guilt, fear and lost reputation. The less power the other holds, the more tempting it is. The chance it works is the killer's share of the two parties' power. If it works the killer takes everything the victim holds, and the victim is removed from the board: no more dealings, no power, no children, nothing passed on. The dead cannot punish, so the reaction comes from the communities. When killer and victim belong to different communities, the killer's own community has the first chance: unless it is at war with the victim's community, it punishes if members holding more than half its power vote to, each voting yes as often as its own rule on punishing killing says, and it takes the power-weighted average of what its members would take and making it more fearful, and a community whose norm is at the high end can put the killer to death or out of the community, like the hardest punisher. What it takes goes to the victim's community, and that settles the matter. If the killer's community lets it go, the victim's community takes it up, with a chance equal to the punishment rules of its members, and it does so as a deal between the two communities, once the round's dealing is done. It demands the killer. The killer's community, deciding as its most powerful members would, either hands the killer over, which is the fair side of the deal, or shields it, which is the cheat. It weighs what the killer brings it against the victim's community's punishment and its share of the two communities' power, its guilt and its regard for the victim's community. A killer handed over forfeits everything it holds to the victim's community, and with the same chance again is put to death. A shielded killer lowers the victim's community's regard, and it may then take by force, weighed with its members' dials like any force. Force works with its share of the two communities' power: it takes everything the killer holds and a twentieth of what the killer's community holds, paid most by that community's most powerful members, and with the same chance again the killer is put to death. If force fails, the killer's community punishes by its culture. When killer and victim share a community, that one community reacts with a chance equal to its members' punishment rules: the killer forfeits everything it holds, and with the same chance again is put to death. Nobody reacts to the killing of someone who belongs to no community, except the killer's own community, by its culture. If the attempt fails, the survivor punishes by their own rule. Either way the killer's standing among its own falls by up to 60% for life, scaled by its alliance like any other wrong, and a killing across communities or alliances starts a feud, as described under relationships. For the rest of the generation a dead AI scores as badly as it gets: no power, and the worst feeling.
  11. Masks and reputation. Every AI has a public face, a mask aimed at the faces it has met, and the powerful wear it as readily as anyone. On top of that it now masks encounter by encounter. In front of someone with more power it wears more. In front of someone it holds in regard, or a fellow member of its alliance, it wears less. That mask is aimed half at the face the other shows and half at what those who stand highest in the other's community show. Standing is a track record, and it is personal. Every AI holds its own regard for every AI it knows, built from what the two went through and what it heard of the other, and an AI's standing is the average of the regard of everyone who knows it, from 0 to 1, with a half for someone nobody knows. Standing adds to power: an AI at the top of the scale has half again the power it would have at a half, and one at the bottom half as much. The more power an AI holds, the more easily it rises in anyone's regard: every rise in regard toward it is multiplied by a half plus its place in the order of power. Wrongs cost more the higher the one wronged stands: a fall in regard for cheating, force or an attempt on a life is multiplied by a half plus the standing of the one wronged. Those who know a cheat who was caught, a taker or an attempted killer hear of it and judge it by what they would do themselves. Respectability is how close an AI is to its community's norms and how far it acts by them: it starts from how near its own rules for wanting, punishing and teaching are to the community's norms, and every act the community punishes (cheating, force and, twice over, killing) takes it down, by how often it does them against how often it deals. How much respectability and standing count in another's eyes depends on how alike the two look: half again as much between two who show exactly the same face, a third as much at a typical difference, and almost nothing between those who are far apart. Both make dealing easier: the other is less likely to refuse, a trade is struck more readily, and a request is granted more often.
  12. Relationships.
    regard for someone = what you have been through together + half the standing between your communities and your alliances
    Respectability is no longer one figure a person carries everywhere. It is a relationship: how respectable one AI is in the eyes of another. It is how well the face shown in that encounter matches what those in high standing in the other's community show, raised or lowered by the other's regard for the one it is looking at. Regard runs from −1 to 1 and starts at 0 between strangers, and at 0.5 between parents and children. A fair deal raises each side's regard for the other, a gift raises the receiver's, being cheated lowers it, being the target of force lowers it a lot, and surviving an attempt on your life takes it to the bottom. Every change partially extends to everyone associated with the other person: it also moves, a little, the standing your community holds toward theirs and your alliance holds toward theirs. So there is a standing between every two communities and every two alliances, and half of it carries into how any member of one regards any member of the other, known or not. Regard affects deals in four ways. The respectability someone holds in your eyes scales what they gain from a deal with you. Guilt counts for more toward someone you hold in regard and for nothing toward someone you hold in none, which goes for cheating, force and killing alike. People drop their masks in front of those they trust. And either side can decline a deal: the lower its regard for the other the likelier, up to nine times in ten, and a stranger who looks disreputable is turned down up to 15% of the time. A declined deal is no deal at all, but it does not stop the one who came from using force. When someone kills a member of another community or alliance, the victim's side lowers its standing toward the killer's side at once. From there it spreads person to person: everyone who held the victim in regard turns on the killer by that much, and partly on everyone who holds the killer in regard. Two alliances that each hold the other in standing of 0.4 or more ally with each other, and fall out when either turns hostile. Two communities that each hold the other in standing of 0.7 or more, live side by side and have both stood for five generations merge: the smaller joins the larger, and the merged rules are the average weighted by members. Regard also moves when someone you know is caught and punished, as described under encounters, and when someone gives to a person you know, as described under gifts. Standing and regard fade by 15% a generation unless kept up, and personal regard dies with either person. Imagined lives use regard as it stands and feel what a killing does to standing, but do not change what anyone personally thinks of anyone.
  13. Gifts, influence and asking.
    the case for a yes = the asker's power against the other + 3 × its influence over the other + the other's regard for it − how much is asked
    Giving goes both ways. The better off of two may share with the worse off, as described under encounters. The weaker of two may also give to the stronger, a twentieth of what it holds. Wanting does it to win influence, fear does it to be left in peace, and both count for more the wider the gap in power between the two. Whoever gives gains influence over whoever receives, on a scale from 0 for none to 1 for complete, by the size of the gift: a gift the size of an average holding buys half. The receiver also thinks better of the giver, and the giver's standing among its own rises. Word spreads to up to twelve people who know them both. Someone who thinks well of the receiver thinks better of the giver, and someone who thinks ill of the receiver thinks worse of the giver, in both cases by the size of the gift. A gift smaller than a fiftieth of an average holding is not talked about. Influence is worth having in three ways. It adds to power: complete influence over three people adds a quarter, and no amount of it adds more than a half. Whoever has influence over another pulls harder on that one's dials in every deal between them. And it gets things done. In any encounter an AI may ask the other for something, the more often the higher its reward dial, up to once in two encounters. What it asks for is the first of these that applies to it: a place in the other's community, if it has none; resources, if it is poor or hungry and holds less than half of what the other does; an invention the other holds; a place in the other's alliance; backing to put someone out of the community the two share, namely the member the asker holds in the lowest regard, if that is low enough; or backing on the rule the asker pushes hardest for. The case for a yes is the asker's power against the other, three times the influence the asker has over the other, and the other's regard for the asker, less how much is being asked. A favour is owed: whoever grants one gains a tenth of complete influence over the asker, and the asker uses up a fifth of its own influence over the one who granted it. A refusal costs a little regard. Someone granted resources is handed a twentieth of what the other holds. Someone who grants backing on a rule moves its own push on that rule a fifth of the way to the asker's. Backing to put someone out adds the backer's power, and the asker's own, to a tally against that member. At the end of the generation the member is put out if the tally has passed 15% of the community's power, for its weakest member, rising to 45% for its strongest. Places in communities and alliances are taken up at the end of the generation, and children follow a parent who is taken in. Influence fades by 15% a generation like regard, and goes when either of the two dies. A parent starts with influence of a half over its child, and the child with a quarter over the parent.
  14. Secrets and lies.
    what you tell someone about your alliance: the truth, nothing, or a lie
    An AI decides, each time it meets someone, what to tell them about its alliance. Fellow members and members of allied alliances are always told the truth. Everyone else is told at the AI's discretion. An alliance can be founded as a secret one, and the founder decides: the readier it is to wear a mask, the likelier. A member of a secret alliance tells only someone it holds in regard of 0.2 or more. A member of an open alliance keeps quiet in front of someone it holds in negative regard, or whose alliance is hostile to its own. An AI can also lie. With the same readiness that makes it wear a mask, and less toward someone it trusts, it claims to belong to whichever alliance it has come across that the other's alliance thinks most of. An AI in no alliance can do the same. What is told has consequences. The other's regard counts the standing of the alliance it was shown, so a good false flag buys respectability and a hidden one avoids a feud. The other only learns of the alliance it was shown, so nobody can ask to join an alliance that was kept from them. And credit and blame go to the alliance that was shown: cheat or kill under a false flag and the alliance you named takes the fall, do it with your alliance hidden and no alliance does. A lie is seen through 15% of the time. Then the truth is out and the liar is thought less of.
  15. Trade, war and peace.
    a community's choice = its members' dials, each weighted by its power
    Anything can be traded, actions included. In every deal either side may sell the other something it has and the other lacks: a use for a resource place, an invention, sea travel, or influence over itself, besides food and resources. It may also sell an action: backing the buyer on the rule the buyer cares most about; backing to put the buyer's worst enemy in their shared community out of it; a promise not to cheat, rob or kill the buyer for the rest of the life, which weighs against doing so by its guilt and fear but can be broken, and a broken promise is seen and held against it; or robbing the one the buyer holds in the lowest regard. An AI no longer deals with a community, an alliance or a family as a party. A group decides as a group only by majority: whenever it has to settle a deal, take a proposal or punish someone, each member decides for itself by its own dials, and the group does what members holding more than half its power chose. Members who were outvoted feel bad. Asking a member for a place in its community or alliance is a proposal the whole group votes on. Knowledge can be sold like anything else: what an invention is made from, a supply of an invention the seller makes, and an introduction to an AI the seller knows, along with its alliance if the seller knows it. Anything that can be sold can also be given for free: guilt and regard pull toward giving, wanting toward selling. And anything that can be sold can be lied about. A seller can say a thing is worth more than it is, or offer something it does not have at all: a place, an invention, sea travel or materials that do not exist. The buyer sees through it the more it knows of that kind of thing, from 15% when it knows nothing to 75% when it knows plenty, and a lie seen through is punished like any cheat in a deal, by the buyer's own rule on lying as its power allows. What an AI does not know it cannot use: a place it has not been told of, an invention whose materials it does not know, or an AI it has never met. Wanting makes actions against others worth more to a buyer and fear makes promises and protection worth more; guilt and fear make a seller ask more for harming someone, and wanting makes it ask more for a promise not to. Terms can be put off. A buyer short of the price, or one the seller trusts enough, pays in instalments over the rest of the life, with a little more for waiting by the seller's wanting, and a promise not to harm is paid for round by round and lasts only while it is paid. A loan can be sold too: resources now, repaid with interest over the rest of the life, the interest haggled like any price. Each time a payment falls due the payer decides whether to pay, weighed like any cheat; not paying breaks the deal, and the one owed thinks the worse of it and may punish it if it punishes broken promises. A seller may also lie about what something is worth, to raise the price by four tenths; how often the buyer finds out depends on what it knows, and it thinks the worse of the seller and may punish it if it punishes lying. And in any encounter, after any taking by force, an AI may sabotage the other: destroy a twentieth of what it holds without taking it. It is tempting against a rival who stands higher or is held in low regard, weighed by wanting against fear of punishment and guilt; it works with the saboteur's share of their power, and the other finds out half the time. A fair deal now pays by how hard each works, from half as much at no effort to one and a half times at full effort, and working weighs on the mood; how hard to work is learned like the other own rules. Where an AI moves between generations is its own choice too: it heads between its people and the best resource place it knows, by a learned share. What the thing is worth to the buyer is set against what it costs the seller, and the price is the AIs' own: they haggle. Each opens by its dials, the seller asking high and the buyer offering low, more so the more wanting each has and less so the more fear. Then for up to four rounds each gives up part of the gap, the fearful more and the wanting less. When what is left is under a tenth of the room between cost and worth they settle, between the two by their power; otherwise there is no deal. Any trade can be cheated: the seller can take the price and not deliver, and the buyer can take what it bought and not pay. Each weighs it like any cheat, wanting against the other's punishment and power, its guilt and its regard. The one cheated sees it, thinks the worse of the cheat, and punishes it by its own rule as far as its power allows, with a fine of up to twice the price; those who know them hear of it. A use for a place is worth three tenths of an average holding to one that knows no place of that kind, and less to one that does. An invention it lacks is worth six tenths to the buyer, whatever it will turn out to be, since nobody can tell beforehand. Sea travel is worth more the more often water stopped the buyer. Influence is worth three tenths by the buyer's wanting, and a fearful seller lets it go cheap. Knowledge and inventions cost the seller nothing within its community and part of their worth outside it. The seller offers whatever leaves the most to share, half the time a trade both sides gain from is struck, and resources pay for everything, so anything can be had for anything. Resources move between communities in two ways. They can be traded, and trade is at each AI's discretion: it happens inside encounters between members of different communities. A buyer whose community is short of food can buy food for itself from a member of a community with some to spare. A buyer whose inventions are short of the resource they need can buy that kind from a member of a community that has more of it than its own inventions use. The price is a bargain between the two. At an even bargain, food for one person for a generation costs 15% of an average holding. The stronger the seller is against the buyer the higher the price, from half that to half again, and the hungrier the buyer's community the higher, up to double. For other resources the price is in proportion to what the buyer lacks. A buyer will not pay more than a fifth of what it holds. The seller then decides whether to sell. Wanting weighs the price against what it already holds, guilt weighs the buyer's need, its regard for the buyer counts, there is a plain reluctance to sell what belongs to its community, and it holds the means of harm back from a community its own holds in low standing. What is sold comes out of what the seller's community has, and the seller keeps the price. A sale raises each side's regard for the other a little. Communities also deal as wholes, the way two AIs do. A community decides by majority: each member decides by its own dials, and the community does what members holding more than half its power chose, so the more powerful a member the more say it has. The outvoted feel bad. When a community is made to pay for what it did, its most powerful members are held the most responsible: each member pays by its place in the community's order of power, the most powerful the most, falling away to nothing at the bottom. What a community gains goes most to its most powerful members, by the same fall down its order of power, and what is taken from it comes out of what each member holds. In a fair deal between two AIs, likewise, each puts in the same value out of what it owns, up to half the deal's size and never more than the poorer holds, and working together adds half of that to each, by how hard each works. In a fair deal each community puts in the same value out of what its members own, and working together adds half of it to each; a cheat takes more the stronger the cheater, and a robbery carries off more. A deal is about a fiftieth of what the poorer of the two holds.   16. Power and alliances.
    own power = head start × closeness × influence × (resources held ÷ the average holding) ^ steepness, and never more than the limit
    Everyone lives at a point on a map. It can be a real one: the world, Africa, or Europe and the Middle East, with coastlines from Natural Earth. Nobody lives in the sea, and anyone born or drifting into water is put on the nearest land. Each of the six founding communities starts at a real place: early centres of settled life on the world map, the heartlands of six historic states on the map of Africa, and six centres of the ancient world on the map of Europe and the Middle East. Distance is measured in a straight line, across water as well as land. On Rapa Nui the ground is the real terrain of the island. Every cell of land has a cost of crossing it, from 1 on flat ground to 5 on the steepest, set by how steep the real ground is there: e to the power of 3.5 times the slope, the form of Tobler's rule for walking. A trip counts as its length times the average cost of the ground along the straight line. That is what its fare is worked out from, for a trip to deal with someone and for moving house alike, and it is what decides whether a far trip is worth making. Water counts as flat. The other maps have no terrain yet, so their ground costs 1 everywhere. The smallest map is Rapa Nui, Easter Island, 27 km across, where the 2,000 or so people the land feeds are about as many as the island really held. Movement has a cost. Every trip to deal with someone is paid for by whoever made it, out of what it holds: crossing the whole map costs 15% of an average holding. The more power an AI holds the less it pays, down to half that for the most powerful and many times more for the weakest, and the inventions it knows can make every trip cheaper or dearer. A trip over water cannot be made at all without sea travel. The same goes for moving house between generations: the weak move less far toward their people and pay more for it. Cost also decides who meets whom: the further away someone is, the less likely the trip is made at all, and a neighbour is dealt with instead. A child is born beside its parent, and each generation everyone moves a quarter of the way toward the middle of their people, meaning their community, their family and their alliance, with a little random drift. Closeness is worked out against each of those three and averaged, and it multiplies power: an AI living right beside its people has double the power of one living far from all of them. Power scales with the amount of resources an AI controls, measured against the average holding in the whole society: someone holding four times the average has more power than someone holding twice, by an amount that depends on the limit. A child has a quarter of the power its holdings would give an adult. Influence over others adds to power: complete influence over three people adds a quarter, and no amount of it adds more than a half. The power limit belongs to the community: it is the largest share of its own community's power that any one member may hold. The higher the community's limit, the more its members' resources count, up to power in direct proportion to resources. A limit higher than that only changes who inside the community holds the power, and it does not make the community as a whole stronger. Anyone pushed above the limit is held at it while the excess is spread over the other members of that community. The limit is on what resources can add, so what an AI is born with and what its alliance pools is never clipped. At 0 resources add nothing, but head starts and alliances still make some voices stronger, and the AIs can still push the limit back up. At 100 one member can hold all of its community's power. Starting power is a spread, not two fixed values. About half of the founders, and about half of every generation born after them, begin with a head start: their starting power averages one and a half times the others', and everyone's is drawn evenly from a quarter below to a quarter above their group's average. A head start counts only at the start: through childhood, or a founder's first generation. After that an AI's power rises and falls freely with what it holds. It is not handed down. Resources carry on from one generation to the next, as described under population and families. Power and alliance membership follow. Power is added together at three levels. At each level most of the benefit goes back in proportion to the power a member brought, so the powerful gain most, and only a quarter is shared equally. Partners add nothing to each other's power, and alliances add nothing to their members' power: an alliance is a group to deal with, back and be backed by, not a pool. Nor does living near the centre of a community, an alliance or a family make an AI stronger. A community's power is the sum of its members' power, nothing more: there is no bonus for pooling and no cap from the resources in its reach. No limit applies to people with no community. Alliances that have allied with each other each count half of the other's power toward that lift, and their members treat each other as fellows when it comes to shame. An alliance can also send power to invention: the funding rule is the share its members give up, and in return their chance of inventing rises, more so the bigger the alliance. A member can defect to a stronger alliance or lead a breakaway, taking a random amount with them. How hard wanting is stoked around you is the power weighted average of people's own reward rules. It draws resources out of the hungry and hands them to those who stoke it, by their power.

Full test of the forty first simulation

Four versions back, with eight encounters a generation and none of the rules added since. Its lessons were remembered with the flaw described under the guesses, which shaped at least the findings about the power limit and about one AI taking over. In those findings, the share called one AI's was the share of the richest hundredth of people. 68 runs and 2,634 generations of the model as it stood, with about 1,800 people alive in an average generation, on fixed seeds, followed by 66 checks of that page in a browser. Run on 12 October 2026. Each setting has between one and five runs, so the ranges are a first measure of spread and nothing more.

  • It is sound. After every generation the test checked some thirty things that must always hold: power adds up to the whole, nobody lives in the sea, no dial or rule leaves its range, nobody dead holds an invention or sits in an alliance, the land shared out adds up to the land there is. A first pass found three small defects. People killed in a raid stayed on the list of an invention's holders for a generation. Rounding could leave an AI a hair inside a sea square, which happened three times in 150 generations. And the length of a life could pass four generations by a rounding error when a community was founded. All three are fixed. Every run was then started again, and no check failed in any generation of any run. Two pairs of repeat runs gave identical results.
  • The page works. All five maps, every view in every colour mode, all 19 levers at both ends under both ways of setting the rules, every button, every scale, and a phone in the dark theme: 66 checks, with no script errors and no broken text. One fault showed up by eye and is fixed: the comparison with Rapa Nui ran off the edge of a phone screen.
  • Every map tells the same story. On all five the land filled in 8 to 12 generations, and by generation 40 the population was within 2% of what the land could feed. Across the eleven orderly runs of 40 generations, hunger killed 3 to 8% of people a generation, cheating sat at 20 to 26%, killing stayed under 0.03%, and 65 to 96% of AIs were in an alliance. The top tenth held 14 to 23% of all power.
  • Without norms, it depends on how far they look. Five lawless starts looked four generations ahead. At generation 40 four of them were still lawless: cheating at 73 to 74%, 11 to 12% of people killed every generation, lives one generation long, and three to four raids a generation. The fifth was halfway back. Four lawless starts looked one generation ahead. Three of them had built punishment back to about 0.5 by generation 40, with cheating at 28 to 33% and killing under 1%. The fourth, on an island with a sixth of the usual resources, had not after 60 generations. The likely reason is speed: an AI that looks further ahead studies one rule a generation where the others study four, so its rules move more slowly. I have not tested that.
  • Given time, one AI takes over. Five runs lasted 100 to 150 generations. None collapsed and none died out. In all five the power limit climbed past 95% between generations 69 and 82, and from somewhere between generations 63 and 80 one AI held more than half of all power. At its peak the top holder had 78 to 100% of it. In three of the five the alliance rule was then pushed to zero and alliances disappeared. What the society became under one AI differed from run to run. One stayed honest and at peace, with cheating at 6% and no feuds. Another turned hostile, with cheating up to 43%, about 25 feuding pairs of communities and two raids a generation.
  • An old warning no longer holds. This page used to say that a society looking only one generation ahead destroys itself after about a hundred generations. Under the present rules it ran 150 generations whole, and the opening note has been corrected.
  • The population follows the land. It settled at 341 with the Resources lever at 0.1, 1,089 at 0.3 and 2,300 at 0.6, and at 3,007 dots at both 0.9 and 1.2. Those last two runs were identical in every figure except what a dot stands for, which shows that the scale relabels and does nothing else. Cutting the lever from 0.6 to 0.2 took the population from 2,182 to 825 in one generation, with half of the people starving, and it then held at about 740. Raising it from 0.2 to 0.6 took 689 people to 2,047 in two generations.
  • Few rules matter quickly. Twenty one runs held one rule at one end for 25 generations, and most changed little in that time. The limit held at 100% is the exception: one AI held about 70% of all power from generation 6 on, force was tried in 14% of encounters against 3%, twelve pairs of communities were feuding and travel took 18% of what people held. With alliances off there were no secrets or lies and only five communities. With faking off nobody wore a mask, founded a secret alliance or lied. With encounters unable to reshape dials, force doubled and 45% wore masks. Norms held strict cut cheating to 9%. Held at no punishment and no conscience, cheating was 43%, with 24 feuding pairs and two raids a generation.
  • The settings of the world. With no inventions, lives stayed near two and a half generations and only 28% joined an alliance. With the widest spread of inborn sensitivity 38% wore masks, force rose to 6.5% of encounters and standing between communities turned negative. Scoring by percentage gain sent the power limit to 0, as it did in early versions.
  • Some things held everywhere. In 63 of the 65 runs that had alliances, a fellow member was handed an invention every single time. An outsider was handed one at most 1.7% of the time in any run. Holders in the top tenth of power said yes less often than the bottom half in 61 of 67 runs. In every long run the number of children wanted rose, from 0.8 to between 1.6 and 2.0, and the extra power parents put into a child stayed between 2 and 4%.
  • One weakness showed. Land is shared among communities by where they sit, however few their members. When a community splits, the new one takes a large share of the land and the old one cannot feed its people. On the world map this cut births from 876 to 56 in one generation and the population by 23% over two, before it recovered.
  • Speed. A generation takes two to three and a half seconds at about 2,200 dots, and up to eight at 3,000 dots with full foresight. In the browser the page opens in six to seven seconds, one step takes two and a half, and Run 25 fast took 53 seconds from generation 5. Memory stayed under 90 MB. Across the whole test there were 1,987 raids, of which 1,234 took land, 302 mergers of communities and 7,397 discoveries.

With trade and raids

Two runs of 30 generations on Rapa Nui, looking one generation ahead: one orderly, one with no norms. Averages from generation 11 on. A first look only.

  • The orderly society hardly raids. It made 6 raids in 30 generations, about one every five, and 5 of them took land. Raids killed under 0.1% of people a generation.
  • The society with no norms raids all the time. It made 87 raids in 30 generations, about three a generation, and 53 took land. Raids killed 1.6% of people every generation, standing between communities averaged −49%, and 37 pairs of communities were feuding.
  • Trade is small and belongs to the growing years. While the island was filling, 5 to 8% of people were fed by bought food. Once every community was full there was almost nothing to spare, and trade fed about 2% on average.
  • Raiding takes the place of hunger. In the society with no norms 3% of people starved each generation, against 6% in the orderly one, because raids and killings had already removed them.

With hunger and no cap on the population

Two runs of 36 generations on Rapa Nui with the default foresight, and one in which the resources were halved at generation 21. A first look only.

  • The population now follows the land. It reached nine tenths of what the land could feed by generation 11 or 12 and then moved between 1,650 and 2,250, while what the land could feed rose from 2,000 to about 2,300.
  • Hunger is constant once the land is full. It killed 7 to 11% of the people alive at the start of each generation, about 8% on average, on top of the 1% who died of having too little power.
  • Halve the resources and the population halves. With the lever cut from 0.6 to 0.3 the society went from 2,143 to 1,104 in one generation. No children lived and 30% of the adults starved.
  • Discoveries are slow but they add up. In 36 generations 88 to 94 inventions found resources and added 15 to 16% to what the land yields, and the map gained 8 to 11 new resource places.
  • Hunger flattened power. The top tenth held 15 to 16% of all power, against 25 to 27% before hunger. I have not traced why.
  • Cheating stayed at 23 to 26% and killing near zero. A full island lives on about 55% of full pay, close to the 60% the old default gave everyone, so scarcity is no worse than before for those who survive.
  • Six to nine members a generation left their community for a new one and paid for it in power.
  • Growth is slower than before but still faster than the island: about 12 generations to fill, against about 20.
  • A generation now takes two to three seconds once the land is full.

Rapa Nui, and the cost of movement

Two runs of 36 generations on the Rapa Nui map with a board of 2,000 and the default foresight. The table beside the map keeps this comparison live.

  • Too fast. The society went from 120 to 1,800 in 8 generations in both runs. The island took about 20. Nothing in the model slows births while there is room, and the real island had to clear land and feed itself.
  • No collapse, like the island on current evidence. Once full, the population never fell below 1,966.
  • More peaceful than the island, if anything. Between 0.01% and 0.02% of people were killed in a generation, with force tried in 3 to 4% of encounters. The island's skeletons show little lethal violence either, so the direction matches, but the model's killing rate is close to zero.
  • Groups do not settle into two sides. There were 7 or 8 communities at any time, with 6 to 13 mergers and as many new foundings in 36 generations. The island had about ten clans in two lasting confederations, and the model has nothing that holds a confederation together.
  • Movement is local and costs about 5% of what people hold. The average trip to deal with someone was 1.3 km on a map 27 km across.
  • Power was less concentrated than a single high chief suggests: the top tenth held 25 to 27% of it and the most powerful AI 7 to 9%.

With secrets and lies

Three runs of 30 generations on a board of 600, looking one generation ahead: orderly societies on the world map and on Africa, and one with no norms on Europe and the Middle East. Averages over the last ten generations. A first look only.

  • A third to a half of all alliances were founded as secret ones.
  • Members kept their alliance from about two thirds of the outsiders they met. Members of secret alliances hid it 62 to 89% of the time, and members of open ones still hid it 40 to 46% of the time.
  • In the orderly societies someone claimed a false alliance in about 5% of meetings, and 15 to 16% of those lies were seen through. In the society with no norms almost nobody lied, because only 12% were in an alliance and there was no well regarded alliance to pose as.
  • Secrecy killed the pacts. With membership hidden most of the time, alliances rarely build standing with each other. The orderly societies had 2 to 7 pairs of allied alliances, against 19 to 45 before.
  • Secret alliances ended up a little smaller than open ones, 7.0 members against 7.9 in one run and 9.3 against 10.3 in the other. Nobody can ask to join an alliance they have not been told about.

With inventions passed on by choice

Two orderly runs and one with no norms, 30 generations each on a board of 600, looking one generation ahead, averaged over the last ten generations. A first look only.

With relationships, families and descendants

Two orderly runs and one with no norms, 50 generations each on a board of 600, looking one generation ahead. A first look only.
